# A Copernican Initiative for Assessing Mathematical Work

[中文](Copernicus.zh.md)

> *Es ist hiemit eben so, als mit den ersten Gedanken des Copernicus bewandt, der, nachdem es mit der Erklärung der Himmelsbewegungen nicht gut fort wollte, wenn er annahm, das ganze Sternheer drehe sich um den Zuschauer, versuchte, ob es nicht besser gelingen möchte, wenn er den Zuschauer sich drehen und dagegen die Sterne in Ruhe ließ.*

— Immanuel Kant, *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*

In my humble view, whatever form mathematics may take in the future, it must ultimately remain human mathematics. Its value should therefore be sought in people: neither conferred by the mysteries of nature nor measured solely by the usefulness of tools. As I reflect on it, mathematics is ultimately a human activity; in research, learning, teaching, and applications alike, people should be at its center. I therefore venture to formulate a “first principle”:

> **Mathematical contributions serve the mathematical community's knowledge and understanding of mathematical objects, structures, and questions.**

On this basis, every assessment of the value of mathematical work can be traced back to what it does for people. What does it add to our knowledge? What does it deepen in our understanding? What methods does it place at our disposal? What mathematics does it make easier for us to understand, check, and use?

Allow me, then, to advocate a “Copernican turn” in mathematical assessment: from asking “How difficult, novel, or complex is this work?” to asking “What does it contribute to human mathematical knowledge and understanding?” Difficulty, novelty, and complexity no longer serve in themselves as grounds for value; their significance should be explained through actual gains in knowledge and understanding. We can distinguish these gains in terms of Knowledge, Understanding, Methods, and Exposition, and then ask further: do they concern local details or crucial difficulties? To what extent do they change the conditions under which we understand and handle the mathematical questions concerned?

Yet how can such assessment be put into practice? Relying on people alone, the time and effort needed to check prior work, trace arguments, and compare gains and limitations can readily leave one task neglected for another. Entrusting everything to AI, however, would mistake its ability to process text for human experience of understanding mathematics. Its ability to reconstruct an article's line of thought does not mean that the article explains it clearly to people; its ability to list many consequences does not mean that those consequences carry substantial weight.

Putting this initiative into practice therefore requires humans and AI to advance it together. AI helps locate relevant prior work, trace argumentative dependencies, compare new contributions, and present both evidence and unresolved questions. Experts familiar with the content examine crucial comparisons and arguments, judging what these gains mean for human understanding and research. Omissions and disagreements found in that examination call for further investigation; neither AI confidence nor human authority can replace reasons.

To distinguish these gains and assess their weight, I have attempted to formulate the following principles as a starting point for discussion.

- **Respect historical context:** Identify a work's contribution relative to existing knowledge and understanding in the research context in which it appeared.
- **Judge the work itself:** Attend to mathematical content and exposition; neither the author's identity nor the fame of the problem can replace a judgment of the specific contribution.
- **Ground the judgment:** Give mathematical reasons open to scrutiny, distinguishing supported judgments from unresolved questions.
- **Value exposition:** Recognize that helping people understand, check, and use mathematics is itself a contribution.
- **Weigh gains and limitations:** Explain advances together with the conditions, costs, and limitations they entail.
- **Leave long-term influence to history:** Leave lasting influence to subsequent research and historical examination; do not count expectations for the future as present achievements.

Under these principles, four kinds of contribution should be distinguished and examined separately:

- **Knowledge:** Which conclusions are added or corrected, which conditions of applicability are clarified, and which grounds for conclusions are strengthened? This concerns what we know and how reliable that knowledge is.
- **Understanding:** Which difficulties are clarified, which connections are revealed, and which mechanisms are explained? This concerns whether we better understand why things are so, beyond knowing that a conclusion holds.
- **Methods:** What means of argument, construction, computation, or verification are supplied, which limitations are overcome, and which costs are reduced? This concerns how we can investigate and handle problems, beyond the results obtained by applying those means.
- **Exposition:** How do presentation, representation, and organization make mathematics easier to understand, check, and use? This concerns how people come to grasp existing knowledge; even without new conclusions or explanations, making them easier to grasp can still constitute a contribution.

These four should be judged separately because they do not always advance together: new conclusions need not bring new understanding, new methods need not immediately produce new conclusions, and existing mathematics may become easier to grasp through better exposition. A work can make several kinds of contribution, but achievement in one cannot substitute for judgment in another.

Only on this basis can we assess the mathematical significance of these contributions: do they merely improve minor details, remove crucial obstacles, or fundamentally change the conditions under which we understand and handle the mathematical questions concerned? Their weight should be explained through these specific changes, rather than by simply adding the first four dimensions. The resulting assessment should make clear what the work adds to existing knowledge and understanding, why those gains matter, and which judgments still require verification.

I tentatively call this proposal the “Copernican Initiative”: to ground mathematical value in human knowledge and understanding, base judgments on concrete mathematics, and advance mathematical assessment through the joint participation of humans and AI. What it asks of mathematical work should also apply to assessment itself: to make its reasons clear, open them to scrutiny, and enable people to think further on that basis.
